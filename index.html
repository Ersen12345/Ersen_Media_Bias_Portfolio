CASE 01 / JIGSAW ASSESSMENT

Visuals & Footage: teaching the class how a photo can lie

Before I could write my own analysis, my Jigsaw group (Angel, Indri, Christofer, Marzio and Mandy) and I had to teach the rest of the class how images and footage shape media bias. Explaining it to others turned out to be its own kind of test.

FIG. 1: same protest movement, framed as chaos

"If we show the negative side of the protest... this makes people forget what the concern actually is."

We built our teaching slides around three real protest photos and asked the class to compare them: one framed as a chaotic crowd blocking a road, one framed as a single raw emotional close-up, and one framed as a calm, organised crowd.

Same movement, three completely different first impressions, depending only on which photo a newsroom decides to run.

My reflection

Honestly, before I had to teach it, I understood "visuals shape bias" as just a fact I'd read somewhere. It didn't really click until I had to explain to Angel, Indri, Christofer, Marzio and Mandy why the close-up hit harder than the wide shot. I couldn't just say "it's more emotional," I actually had to work out the reasoning out loud. Closer framing cuts out context, and context is what lets you judge a situation for yourself, so zooming in doesn't just make something feel more intense, it quietly takes away your ability to see the bigger picture. Watching the group react to that made me realize bias isn't one big decision. It's a bunch of small ones, like crop, distance, which photo gets picked, that only look like a single choice from the outside.

CASE 02 / INDIVIDUAL ASSESSMENT

"30 years of civil war in Somalia", BBC News, 2010

A BBC correspondent films from the front line while a man quietly prays a few metres away. One frame, filed as evidence.

FIG. 2: 1:16 timestamp, BBC News, "30 years of civil war in Somalia"

"We're right on the front line here but it's cracking all around us, and there's a man here quietly praying."

The reporter uses "we're" to pull the audience onto the front line with him. His visible fear at the sound of gunfire, and the man calmly praying beside him, turn a single moment into the emotional center of the whole clip.

Point

Language and framing put the audience directly on the front line, not at a safe distance.

Evidence

First-person "we're," descriptive detail ("cracking all around us"), and a static close-up of a man praying mid-gunfire.

Explanation

It makes the danger feel personal and immediate: the audience isn't told the war is serious, they're made to feel it.

Point

The BBC's credibility as an established, trusted broadcaster gives the footage automatic authority.

Evidence

Filmed first-person, five years before I watched it, under a globally recognised name.

Explanation

The name alone can make an audience trust the framing without questioning what's outside the shot.

Overall verdict

The BBC didn't show everything. It chose civilian suffering over history or war strategy. Choosing empathy over politics isn't a lie, but it is a choice, and it's not balanced between emotion and analysis.

My reflection

Breaking down that one clip changed how I watch war footage because now I can't ignore the gap between what a camera shows and what's actually happening. Before this, "we're on the front line" would've just felt dramatic to me. Now I hear it as a decision the reporter made, to make me feel like I'm there instead of actually explaining what's going on. I catch myself asking what a clip isn't showing, like there's no map, no numbers, no political background, just one man praying and one scared reporter.

CASE 03 / TED-STYLE TALK

Will AI take your job by 2030?

My recorded talk analysing a BBC News article on AI and employment, and the bias hiding in whose voice counts as evidence.

FIG. 3: opening hook still from my recorded talk

"If AI has taken over millions of jobs by 2030, will our generation be prepared, or will we be replaced before we even get started?"

My talk analyses "Will your job be replaced by AI? Here are the roles most affected", BBC News, 22 July 2026, built on employment data from Stanford and the OECD.

CLAIM

Formally balanced, substantively imbalanced

It looks neutral on the surface, but the weight of evidence pushes readers toward one conclusion: AI is a serious threat to jobs, especially for people our age.

SOURCES
  • Named sources are institutions: Stanford, the OECD, BBC's own staff.
  • People losing jobs appear only as statistics: 2.7% overall, rising to 12.8% in the most exposed sectors.
  • No interview with an actual 24-year-old developer told their career path is shrinking.
BALANCE

The counterargument (that interest rates, not AI, explain some job losses) gets one sentence. The "AI disruption" narrative gets paragraphs, charts and quotes. Both sides are mentioned; they are not weighted the same.

Final takeaway

Media bias isn't always lying. Sometimes it's just a choice about what to include, and who gets to speak. This article gave voice to institutions and data, not to the people whose lives are actually changing. That doesn't make it fake. It makes it incomplete.

My reflection

This is my revised script. My first draft basically just summarized the BBC article. I restated the stats, restated the charts, and thought that counted as analysis. What changed it was realizing summarizing someone else's article isn't the same as actually arguing something. So I rewrote the middle part to say the real point clearly: that the article looks balanced but isn't, once you look at who gets named as a source and who's just a number. I also cut a bunch of my own opinions about AI in general because they were pulling attention away from the actual bias I was supposed to be tracking.

FINAL REFLECTION

Closing the case

What three separate investigations taught me about where bias actually hides.

"The most powerful bias isn't the one you see. It's the one you don't notice, hidden in who gets counted and who gets to speak."

CASE 01 TAUGHT MEBias can hide in a single photo choice: chaos or calm, same protest.
CASE 02 TAUGHT MEBias can hide in what a camera lingers on, and what it never shows.
CASE 03 TAUGHT MEBias can hide in who counts as a "source" and who's just a statistic.
Your turn: biggest learning achievement

What surprised me most across all three cases is that none of them involved anyone actually lying. A real photo, a reporter who was genuinely scared, real data from Stanford and the OECD, everything was technically accurate. The bias was never in the facts, it was in what got picked: which photo, which five seconds of footage, whose name gets used versus who just becomes a percentage. That's what I meant calling this whole thing "mysterious and hidden in shadows." You can't catch someone lying, it's built out of a hundred small editorial choices that each seem fine on their own, and you only notice the pattern once you start actually looking for it. Going forward, I'm going to ask a second question when I read the news: not just "is this true," but "who decided this was the angle, and who got left out of it."
